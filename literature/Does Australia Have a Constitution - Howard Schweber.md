#### Notes
- Function of a Constitution  
    - A constitution distills a community's beliefs into a set of rules that govern them.  
    - Constitutions can be explicit, spelling out specific rules in detail, or implicit, leaving room for interpretation and adaptation to societal changes.  
        - Benefits of an explicit constitution: Provides clear and detailed rules.  
        - Drawbacks of an explicit constitution: Tends to be more brittle in the face of social change.  
        - Benefits of an implicit constitution: Adaptable over time to changes in society.  
        - Drawbacks of an implicit constitution: Ambiguity can lead to differing interpretations.

- Types of Constitutions  
    - Constitutions can take two primary forms:  
        - Powers Constitution: Lays out the structure of government and describes how political contestation will be channeled.  
        - Rights Constitution: Lays out the rights of groups and individuals to protect them from overreaches by the government.  
    - Distinction between positive and negative liberties:  
        - Positive liberty: Proactive measures by the government or institutions on your behalf.  
        - Negative liberty: Protection from actions by the government or others.  
        - Note: there are different ways to approach these terms definitionally, based on whether you want their definition to turn on the role of the government, or the role played by others vis-a-vis your own rights or person.   
        - Another way to think of a negative liberty is as a right that you might have possessed unfettered had you been born into a State of Nature.  
            - It may also be worth considering rights that emerge as a result of the political system, and which did not precede it. Justice Breyer's book "Active Liberty" deals somewhat with this idea. The idea underpinning this is that there are some liberties that we would not necessarily describe as positive liberties, but which are secured nonetheless by the mere existence of society and its institutions.   
                - In light of the ideas above, ask yourself: what kind of right is the Right to Vote?   
        - You can theoretically perceive of the same species of rights has having both negative and positive manifestations.  
            - Think of freedom of speech. When the government does not restrict your speech, they are manifesting a negative liberty; when the government creates venues for people to speak, then they are manifesting a positive liberty.

  
- Layers of a Constitutional Regime  
    - Constitutions have different layers that transform them into a constitutional regime, which I'd like us to define as a fully implemented and enforceable set of legal institutions.  
    - Constitutional Ethos:  
        - The zeitgeist, worldview, or social goals motivating the constitution's initial development.  
        - Not fixed in time and evolves with society.  
    - Constitutional Text:  
        - The written document, which must be read and interpreted by people.  
    - Constitutional Norms or Conventions:  
        - Socially agreed-upon approaches that help interpret and implement the constitutional text.  
        - Norms evolve over time and interact with an evolving social ethos, allowing the constitutional regime to adapt without necessarily changing the text.

- Understanding Changes Over Time  
    - A good thought experiment: Is the American Republic of today the same as the American Republic of 1787?  
        - On paper, perhaps relatively little has changed, but major events in American history have led to significant changes in social organization and political institutions.  
    - Notable events may have involved major changes to the ethos and conventions of the American Constitutional regime include:  
        - The Constitutional Convention & passage of the Bill of Rights  
        - The American Civil War  
        - The Great Depression and Second World War   
        - The September 11th attacks and the Patriot Act  
    - A recommended book: *We the People Vol. 2: Transformations* by Bruce Ackerman.
