#### Discussion Prompt

Before class, I had circulated the following prompt to get you thinking about this reading: Epps' whole shtick is that the expansion of civil rights in the 20th century was closely tied to the increased prevalence, sophistication, and activity of advocacy groups which not only provided legal services but also lobbied, influenced public policy, and held the state and federal governments accountable through various forms of activism and strategic litigation. These, he argues, were able to leverage an increasingly robust support structure for legal mobilization, which make up far more than just the advocacy groups themselves. When you read, ask yourself what elements & sectors of society were instrumental (according to Epps) to the development of this structure. I would like you to think about some of the advocacy groups that you know and consider how they might fit into Epps' framework.

#### Support Structure for Legal Mobilization

What is the structure of the legal process, typically speaking? Typically, one party (sometimes the government) needs to sue / bring charges against another. Howard Schweber started his lecture by asking us whether we think this system is best for finding truth? Are paid lawyers a good thing? What if you don't have the money? What if you lack the sophistication to engage in effective legal strategy? What if you lack media connections or a national platform? Are you out-of-luck?

What comprises what Epps calls the "support structure for legal mobilization"? This kind of approach acknowledges that Rights are not just secured in the courtroom. Rather, there are multiple fields of battle: courts, media & democracy, legislative & executive branches. This requires LOTS OF MONEY (donors) too.
