#### Discussion Questions
Douglass North advances, in the opening lines of his paper, a definition of institutions as ...

> ... the humanly devised constraints that structure political, economic and social interaction. They consist of both informal constraints (sanctions, taboos, customs, traditions, and codes of conduct), and formal rules (constitutions, laws, property rights). Throughout history, institutions have been devised by human beings to create order and reduce uncertainty in exchange.

Put more plainly, institutions are sets of rules. This is an abstract definition, and I'd like you to consider how this might differ from what you might think of when you imagine an institution. For most, they think of institutions as something a little more concrete and tangible, eg. a governing organization like a federal agency. But, North's definition allows us to characterize a wider set of things as institutions. Is the English language an institution, in your mind? How about under North's definition? How strictly must these rules be enforced? On one hand, we have systems that enforce rules aggressively; on the other, some rules may play out more like norms or suggestions.  
  
Ultimately, North's paper is focused on economics but discusses history in a way that is very useful, describing how changes to economic relationships and technology led to changes in western society's goals. I would like you to be ready to articulate what some of these shifting goals were, and to take a stab at categorizing the different eras of history relevant to his analysis. I'd like you to read North's definition of path dependence on electronic page 13, which argues that institutions today shape the option set for institutions tomorrow. How does that tie into the law?  
  
How does this paper fit into our other discussions and readings? Edmund Morgan and his legal fictions? Charles Epps and his structure for legal mobilization? Howard Schweber's description of juries and custom early in the common law?

#### Notes
- Today, we discussed a theoretical framework that can help us accomplish two things.
    - First, it can help us think about and categorize institutions in ways that move beyond thinking of them as mere governing institutions (like the Department of Defense or European Union) and embrace institutions more broadly as a set of rules that vary in the extent of their formality. This, in turn, is a useful way for thinking about the concerns facing most lawyers, which often extend far beyond the letter of the law to include other concerns, needs, and expectations.
    - Second, it can help us think through different periods of Western history in ways that help us understand how different laws and legal regimes are situated to pursue different social goals.
- What is an institution?  
    - Rules. According to Doug North, an institution is merely a cluster of rules. So, while a governing institution is definitely an institution, there are other, more abstract things that might still count. Ultimately, institutions are rules that govern our interactions with one another, so they are social in nature. They exist for a whole host of reasons: to protect rights, channel dispute, prevent retaliation for perceived wrongdoings - to name just a few.
    - Enforcement. Enforcement can come in many forms. In its most obvious form, enforcement looks like criminal legal proceedings brought against a defendant for alleged violation of law. But, beyond that, it becomes more complex. It can look like social stigma, influencing others, or threats of secondary consequences for noncompliance with norm. The point to take away from this is that enforcement need not look like typical criminal enforcement to still count.
    - Formality. Institutions vary in their formality based on factors like the extent of their specificity and enforcement.
    - Norms. Institutions that are less specific or less enforced might fall into a category called norms. These are general behavioral conventions that are least likely to be enforced through law.
    - Path dependence. This is more of a theoretical point that North is making. You don't need to worry about it too much, but I think it's a really useful observation. Essentially: the options available to us tomorrow will be based on the institutions (or rules we play by) today. Put another way, it is "cheaper" to follow along the path that existing institutions have charted out for us than trying to innovate new rules from scratch.
    - Abstraction. It's helpful but not necessary to think of institutions in terms of boxes or categories of increasingly complex things. For example, the University of Wisconsin-Madison is an example of an institution with its own rules, sure, but it is also a subset of a broader class of universities (can we call this category "Academia") that all play by a similar (or even completely the same) set of rules. The process of grouping things within a broader, more-generally-defined class is called _abstraction_ and it's useful but limited in a varieties. On the one hand, it allows us to generalize and manage the number of details we need to balance in our mind; on the other hand, sometimes the devil is in the details. In computer science, you might call the abstraction a "class" (eg. Academic) and a specific example of that class as an "instance" (eg. UW-Madison)

- What were the social goals that institutions embodied during different periods of western history, according to Doug North?
    - Feudal era. Hierarchy and security
    - Colonial era. Long-distance trade and trust
    - Industrial revolution. Facilitating economic growth
    - Postwar period. Managing social and economic complexity. Protect rights. Ensure a working society.
- I think we should also be mindful of something called Transaction Costs. These are the costs, financial, social, or otherwise, that people and firms bear by participating in society. In each of the historical eras listed above, we should think of the motivating social goals, and the institutions derived from these, as attempting to reduce Transaction Costs. In fact, another way to characterize institutions, according to North, are as structs that reduce the Transaction Costs of social, political, and economic interaction.
- We should also think about the role of technology. We should ask whether this falls within the framework of "path dependence" that I noted above, or whether technology disrupts the claim of path dependence. One must imagine that, depending on the circumstances, it could go either way.

Tying things all together, I want you to consider this framework when you examine a legal regime or on-the-ground realities that our outside speakers describe. My hunch is that it is a very useful way of thinking about law, both the very high-level course question "what are the social concerns and public policy goals underpinning the law?" as well as the more specific, concrete course question "how does the law really work?".
