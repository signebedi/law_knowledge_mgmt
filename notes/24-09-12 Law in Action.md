- Key takeaways
    - Agency relationships can be relatively easy to form but carry a great deal of risk
    - When in doubt about the proper form for your (or a friend's) business: go with an LLC
    - You should really be mindful of how your actions and decisions in your life open you up to litigation risk
- An agency relationship is a fiduciary relationship where a principal and agent assent to the agent acting on the principal's behalf in ordewr to benefit the principal.
    - See definition and subsequent discussion in Restatement (third) on Agency: Sec. 1.01 [https://www.fiduciarylawblog.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/11/Restatement-101.pdf](https://www.fiduciarylawblog.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/11/Restatement-101.pdf)- - [Links to an external site.](https://www.fiduciarylawblog.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/11/Restatement-101.pdf)
    - A fiduciary duty carries a number of duties with it
    - A principle should be able to exert some control over the agent ... but how much control is necessary for the court to make a finding that an agency relationship existed?
    - This is an important consideration for the purpose of vicarious liability: the principle that an employer will be liable for the actions of their employee.
    - Put another way: an agent is someone who can seriously impact (for better or for worse) the legal relationships of the principal.
    - Hiring independent contractors is a common - but not fool proof - way that employers try to skirt this liability risk. But, courts will often choose to recognize an independent contractor as an employee if the employer exerts enough control in-fact over the time and manner of the alleged contractors work.
    - Put another way: merely calling someone an independent contractor is not sufficient to limit your liability. They must actually be an independent contractor.
    - Legitimate independent contractors have tons of freedom to make choices about their own time and conduct. A gardener or builder are good examples of lines of work well suited to independent contractors.
- When forming a business, you create many agency relationships. The tradeoffs between business forms generally come down to balancing flexibility and formality (the more formalities required by a business form, the more costly in time and money it will be to administer it) and liability risk.
    - A sole proprietorship and partnership are both very flexible, but provide essentially no liability shield. That means you could be totally liable for your business partner's negligent conduct, though you might be able to sue your partner after the fact to recoup their portion of the  liability.
    - Corporations provide liability protections for their shareholders and boards; further, ownership stakes are easily transferred in a corporation. However, they require a higher degree of sophistication than other businesses - eg. you need a Board of Directors, frequent meetings, and all sorts of other filings and requirements that make these undesirable for small businesses.
    - LLCs tend to provide a good balance between the flexibility of partnerships and the limited-liability of corporations.
